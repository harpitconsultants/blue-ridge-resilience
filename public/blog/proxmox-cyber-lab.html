Building My Cybersecurity Research Lab with Proxmox

Building an isolated cybersecurity research lab with Proxmox, a GL.iNet travel router, WireGuard, and hardware I already had lying around.

Crystal Litaker • 8 min read

Purpose of the Lab

The purpose of this lab is pretty simple. I want somewhere that I can research, test, break things, and learn without putting my home network at risk.

The lab will not only be used for capturing and analyzing cyber events. I plan to use it for reverse engineering, threat hunting, use case development, SIEM engineering, detection engineering, testing tools, and pretty much everything in between.

This is also not meant to be some huge enterprise lab. It is something I built with hardware I already had lying around and decided to make useful.

The Setup

The first thing I wanted was to separate the lab from the rest of my home network.

For that I am using a GL.iNet travel router. The travel router sits between my home network and the lab and gives me a separate network for all of my research.

From there, the main system in the lab is an HP ProBook G2 running Proxmox VE as the hypervisor.

The ProBook has been upgraded to:

  • 1TB SSD
  • 16GB RAM
  • Proxmox VE

It is definitely not the most powerful lab server in the world, but that is also kind of the point. You do not need a rack full of enterprise hardware to start building a cybersecurity lab.

I had the laptop available, so I decided to see what I could do with it.

Remote Access

I also wanted to be able to work on the lab when I am not sitting at home.

For remote access I am using WireGuard VPN. This allows me to connect back into the lab when I am away without exposing the Proxmox management interface directly to the Internet.

This is especially useful because I can work on something, leave the house, and still get back into the environment if I need it.

What Is Running in the Lab?

Right now I have several different systems planned or running in Proxmox. This is not a complete list and will probably continue changing as the lab grows.

Splunk and Elastic

Both Splunk and Elastic will be used for collecting and analyzing data.

A big part of this lab is going to be SIEM engineering and detection development. I want to be able to generate activity in one part of the lab, collect the telemetry, and then actually see what that activity looks like from the defender side.

This also gives me somewhere to build and test searches, detections, dashboards, and use cases without worrying about breaking anything important.

Kali Linux and ParrotOS

I am using Kali Linux and ParrotOS for offensive security testing and generating activity inside the lab.

Having both the offensive and defensive side in the same environment gives me the ability to perform an action and then turn around and see what evidence that action created.

That is one of the things I really want to explore more with this lab.

REMnux and FlareVM

For malware analysis and reverse engineering, I am using REMnux and FlareVM.

REMnux gives me a Linux based environment for malware analysis while FlareVM gives me a Windows based reverse engineering environment.

These will eventually be used for analyzing suspicious files, looking at malware behavior, and learning more about the artifacts that malware leaves behind.

Honeypots and Internet Monitoring

I also plan on experimenting with honeypots and other systems that allow me to capture and study activity coming from the Internet.

One of the resources I will be using for this is the SANS Internet Storm Center.

I have followed the Storm Center for years and I want to incorporate some of those ideas into the lab so I can collect activity myself and compare what I am seeing with what others are seeing across the Internet.

You can find the SANS Internet Storm Center at isc.sans.edu .

Resource Limitations

One important thing to note is that all of these systems are not running at the same time.

The ProBook only has 16GB of RAM, so there is obviously a limit to how much I can run at once.

If I am working with Splunk, I may not need FlareVM running. If I am doing malware analysis, I may shut down some of the other systems. It really just depends on what I am working on at the time.

There are plans to eventually upgrade the lab to something more robust. For now, though, this is what I had lying around and I wanted to make it useful.

I also think there is something fun about seeing how much you can actually do without buying a bunch of new hardware.

Blue Ridge Lab Notes

You do not need enterprise hardware to start learning.

  • Keep research systems separated from your normal network.
  • Use what you already have before spending money on new hardware.
  • Build both the offensive and defensive sides of the lab.
  • Collect telemetry so you can see what your activity actually looks like.
  • Do not expect to run every VM at the same time on limited hardware.
  • Let the lab grow as you find new things you want to learn.

What's Next?

This is a limited list of what will be in the lab because it is going to continue growing and changing.

There are a lot of things I want to experiment with, and I am sure that list will change as I find new tools, new projects, and probably new rabbit holes to go down.

As I build things out, I plan on documenting them here.

That includes what worked, what did not work, how I configured things, detections I build, interesting activity I find, and anything else that might be useful to someone building their own lab.

The goal isn't to build the perfect cyber lab.

The goal is to have somewhere to keep learning.
